Cable-Driven Parallel 3D Concrete Printer

A large-scale robotic printing platform combining cable-driven motion, custom spool and extrusion hardware, industrial controls, operator interfaces and physical concrete-printing trials.

Motion ControlServo SystemsHMIIndustrial ElectronicsR&D
Physical print validation Concrete layer and corner test produced during development of the cable-driven printing platform.

Engineering objective

Develop and integrate the controls, motion hardware and extrusion subsystems needed to turn a cable-driven mechanical concept into a testable concrete-printing platform.

My contribution

I handled electronics and control integration, wiring, motor/drive bring-up, spool and extrusion R&D, tower-control interface work, testing and troubleshooting.

Validation outcome

The integrated R&D platform progressed to physical concrete-printing trials that documented layer deposition, corner geometry, wall infill and stacked layer profiles.

Engineering flow

Operator Interface→ Motion Controller→ Motor / Drive Hardware→ Cable / Spool Motion→ End Effector / Auger→ Concrete Print
Project overview

This R&D project combined cable-driven motion, industrial electronics, custom mechanical subsystems and concrete extrusion into a large-scale robotic printing platform. My work focused on controls and electronics integration, motion hardware, subsystem development, operator interfaces, troubleshooting and physical print validation.

Case 01

Electrical, drive and motion-control integration

The platform started with practical integration work: connector preparation, field wiring, drive connections and multi-axis motion hardware assembled into a working R&D system.

Motor-driver connectionsStepper-driver connectors and control wiring were prepared as part of the motion-system build.
Wiring qualityColor-coded conductors were terminated with ferrules into the control connector for a clean, serviceable connection.
Servo integrationServo motor connections were integrated with the servo drive as the higher-power motion hardware was brought online.
R&D hardware cartA development cart brought together a 24 V power supply, servo motor, gearbox, Galil 8-axis motion controller and project wiring.

Case 02

Spool and cable-drive mechanism R&D

The cable-driven architecture depended on translating motor rotation into controlled cable movement, so spool geometry and the motor-to-spool mechanism were developed and tested as their own subsystem.

Prototype spoolA 3D-printed R&D spool was used during mechanism development alongside the ball-screw / geared arrangement and bench measurement equipment.
Servo / spool assemblyA servo motor was mechanically attached to the spool assembly for motion-system development and testing.

Spool Development Evidence

Case 03

End-effector extrusion development

The suspended end effector needed a practical way to move concrete through the print head, leading to screw-auger development for the extrusion subsystem.

Material deliveryA screw-auger mechanism formed part of the end-effector material-delivery hardware.
Iterative R&DMultiple physical views were documented while developing the auger and its integration into the printing head.

Case 04

Tower control interface

Operator-facing controls supported tower-related setup and system interaction during R&D, connecting the physical motion hardware to a usable control workflow.

GUIA dedicated tower-control GUI provided the operator interface used during development.

Case 05

Concrete-printing validation

The project progressed from subsystem development into physical printing trials, allowing layer deposition, corners, wall geometry and infill behavior to be evaluated on real concrete prints.

Layer & corner testAn isometric print documents deposited layers and a formed corner during validation.
Wall infillA separate test print documents wall and infill geometry.
Side profileA side-view test documents the stacked layer profile from another print trial.

Print-Test Evidence

A collaborator’s public project article reports a 14-month development effort and approximately 2–2.5 mm printing accuracy; those figures are attributed to that article rather than presented here as my independent measurement.
